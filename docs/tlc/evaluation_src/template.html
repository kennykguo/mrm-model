ECE496 capstone · evaluation report · draft for review

The ECE496 thermal locking controller on the G26 transmitter model

We moved the thermal locking controller (TLC) that our team designed on a simple 4-ring plant into mrm-model, an independent, higher-fidelity simulator of an 8-ring microring transmitter, and measured how well it keeps every ring locked: first exactly as designed, then with four disclosed adaptations for this plant. This page gives the method, the results, the diagnosis behind them, and the decisions we need.

Summary

The port is sound: the controller code in mrm-model is bit-for-bit identical to the team's source and reproduces its published results. Run exactly as designed, it tracks slow disturbances at about 2 pm rms but loses lock under fast or large ones and locks onto the wrong laser line at start-up. We traced those failures to six causes, four of them specific to this plant, and built a tuned TLC that changes four things, each disclosed and switchable. The tuned TLC holds every slow scenario within mrm's 5 pm budget and matches mrm's own controllers under the aggressor and squelch; it stays behind them in quiet steady state because a dithering controller always carries its own small dither.

1Objective and scope

The TLC was designed and validated only on the team's own purpose-built 4-ring plant. The question is whether it works on a plant it was not designed against. mrm-model provides one: a physics model of an 8-ring O-band transmitter (G26 preset) with its own thermal network, optics, monitor ADC and heater DAC.

  • Faithful arm: the controller exactly as published, adapted only at its interfaces. This is the evaluation of the team's design.
  • Tuned arm: four a-priori changes for this plant, each derived from a diagnosed cause rather than fitted to a score, and reported beside the faithful numbers, never instead of them.
  • Deliverable: one command (tools/tlc_run_all.sh, about 45 min, 151 runs) that runs the full scenario matrix for both arms and every comparator and writes the report.

2System under test

The controller in brief

Each heater carries a small tone (the dither). Demodulating the monitor photodiode at the tone gives the slope of the ring's resonance (z1) and at twice the tone its curvature (z2). Their ratio ζ = z2/z1 does not depend on optical power, so an unscented Kalman filter (UKF) uses it, together with the photodiode's DC level, to estimate each ring's detuning and an optical power scale. A model-predictive controller (MPC) then sets the heaters, and a supervisor handles start-up (ACQUIRE), tracking (TRACK), loss of lock, squelch and fallback to a PI loop.

3Method

3.1 Faithful port and fidelity gate

The controller is copied, not rewritten. The only edits are packaging (relative imports) and making the ring count a parameter. Two automated checks guard this: the copied core must produce bit-identical outputs to the source on the source's own plant, for both sine and triangle dither, and it must reproduce the source's published steady-state (R1), aggressor (R3) and recovery (R4) results. Both pass, so any poor faithful number belongs to the design, not to the port.

3.2 Controller parameters from the nominal design

The controller's plant constants (heater gains and time constants, crosstalk, linewidth, monitor noise) are derived from the G26 nominal design model, never from the simulated world, exactly as mrm does for its own model-based controllers. Examples: heater paths split into a fast and a slow pole; crosstalk 3 % to the neighbour and 1.5 % beyond; monitor noise 3.9 × 10⁻⁵ of full scale.

3.3 Scenario matrix and comparators

Each ECE496 requirement (R1–R22) is mapped to an mrm scenario: steady state, die-temperature ramp, laser drift, local and common aggressors, a +3 dB laser power step, squelch (modulation off), a +10 K kick, a photodiode dropout, a forced fallback and cold start. Disturbances G26 does not model natively are added as labelled extensions. Every scenario runs with both TLC arms, the TLC's own PI baseline and mrm's four controllers (PI, dither lock-in, Kalman-LQG, MPC); cold start compares against mrm's WDM supervisor.

3.4 Metrics and pass bars

Error is each ring's detuning minus mrm's lock reference, in picometres (1 GHz ≈ 5.7 pm at 1310 nm), graded against mrm's 5 pm budget and the ECE496 2 GHz ≈ 11.4 pm threshold. We report rms and peak per ring, and a "tracking" error with the controller's own dither tones removed.

3.5 Statistics and robustness

Rates such as wrong-line locking use exact 95 % Clopper–Pearson intervals. Robustness is tested on four alternative plants ("corners": fast, slow and textbook thermal, TEC-held package), once with controllers told the corner's model and once with the nominal model on the corner plant (a deliberate model mismatch). Most scenario cells are one simulated world; the cold-start campaigns use 8 random worlds.

3.6 How the tuned arm was built

We probed the faithful controller's internal state (estimates, power scale, mode, heater command) against the plant's true detuning in each failing scenario, named a cause for each failure, and changed only what that cause called for. Changes that did not help were dropped and are listed in §5.

4Results

4.1 Every scenario, every controller

As designed, the TLC tracks slow disturbances at 1.8–2.6 pm rms, about 100 times mrm's Kalman-LQG and MPC, and loses lock under the local aggressor and the kick. Tuned, it drops to about 0.55 pm rms and 0.9–3 pm peak, holds the aggressor (0.84 pm rms on rings 1–7 against mrm PI's 0.63) and recovers from the kick. The power step and the stronger PD-side aggressor are lost by almost every controller: those are limits of this plant, not of one design.

4.5 Dither waveform: sine vs triangle

The team's 20 Nov update adds waveforms that are cheap to generate on an FPGA (square, 3-level, triangle); on the 4-ring plant the triangle beat sine (0.55 vs 0.91 GHz steady). These waveforms need tone frequencies of exactly 100 kHz ÷ an even number. No set of 8 such tones avoids interfering with itself, so on G26 the source's 4 tones (3.8–7.1 kHz) repeat every 4 rings; crosstalk 4 rings apart is about 10⁻⁴ and does not matter. The "sine · sampled tones" arm uses the same tones with a sine wave, separating the tone change from the waveform change. This comparison uses the faithful arm.

7Questions for review

Is the tuned arm a fair second result?Each change follows a diagnosed cause and none was fitted to a score, but they change the design. We report both arms side by side.
Which pass bar is the headline: 5 pm or 11.4 pm?As designed, the TLC passes 11.4 pm and fails 5 pm in the same slow scenarios; tuned, it passes both.

8Next steps

  • Demo: show both arms; sine dither.
  • Power step: detect the fall-off and re-acquire fast enough to re-lock.
  • Squelch: give the controller the squelch state, or freeze it when its dither response collapses.
  • Statistics: 300-world cold-start campaign for the tuned arm; more worlds per scenario.
  • Upstream: merge mrm-model's main once its engine is fixed; ask for the ECE496 results to be regenerated.

Future direction: FPGA emulation

mrm-model already has a binary, tick-locked interface for external controllers (mrm/sim/bridge.py), fixed-point helpers (mrm/control/fxp.py) and test-vector export for HDL testbenches. A hardware version of the controller can therefore be tested against the simulator in lockstep: the simulator sends each monitor sample block, the FPGA returns heater codes. This proves the logic and fixed-point behaviour, not real-time timing, because the simulator runs about 15× slower than real time. A sensible order is a fixed-point Python model, then Verilator/cocotb against recorded vectors, then the FPGA over the bridge. The dither, lock-in and PI loop map naturally to hardware; the UKF and MPC are better kept on a soft processor at first.

B. Glossary

Detuning and lock reference

A ring modulates well only when its resonance sits at a fixed offset from its laser line. Heat shifts the resonance by tens of pm per kelvin; the heater pulls it back. The lock reference is the offset mrm considers ideal; our error is the distance from it.

Dither and lock-in detection

A small heater tone makes the photodiode signal wobble. Multiplying by the tone and averaging (lock-in) extracts the wobble's size and sign, which tells which side of the resonance the ring is on. Each ring needs its own tone, chosen so that no tone, harmonic or mixing product lands near another ring's tone.

UKF and MPC

The unscented Kalman filter fuses the lock-in ratio and the DC photodiode level with a thermal model to estimate each ring's detuning, drift and optical power scale. The model-predictive controller plans heater commands a few steps ahead using the same model.

Self-heating and the hot branch

Light absorbed in the ring heats it and pulls its resonance further toward the laser's red side. On G26 this is strong enough that a ring has two stable positions (the hot and the cold branch); losing power or overshooting makes it jump between them by close to a nanometre.

Wrong-line lock

All 8 rings see all 8 laser lines. A ring that locks to a neighbour's line looks locked to the controller but carries the wrong channel.

Corners and priors

Corners are alternative plants. "Corner priors" tells the controller the alternative model; "nominal priors" keeps the design model on the alternative plant, which tests model mismatch.

Clopper–Pearson interval

An exact confidence interval for a rate measured from a small number of trials. 1 failure in 64 trials gives 0.04 %–8.4 %, which is why larger campaigns matter.
